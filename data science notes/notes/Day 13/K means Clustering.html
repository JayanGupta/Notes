Introduction

What is Clustering?

Clustering is the task of grouping a set of data points such that points in the same group (a cluster) are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. It is one of the core techniques of unsupervised learning — the model discovers structure without being told the "right answer" in advance.

Section 2

Understanding K-Means

What is K-Means?

K-Means is a centroid-based partitioning algorithm that divides data into $K$ distinct, non-overlapping clusters, where $K$ is chosen in advance. Developed by Stuart Lloyd (1957) at Bell Labs for pulse-code modulation, its near-linear complexity makes it a workhorse for large datasets.

Intuition — The Pizza Hub Analogy

Imagine opening 3 pizza shops in a city to minimize the average distance customers travel. You place them randomly, deliver to whoever is closest to each shop, then relocate every shop to the center of the customers it currently serves. Repeat — the shops "settle" into the natural centers of demand.
🍕

Optimizing centroid location = optimizing service

How Clusters Are Formed

Every point is assigned to the cluster whose centroid it is closest to (by Euclidean distance), which carves the space into Voronoi cells. Centroids are then recomputed as the mean of the points assigned to them. This assign → update loop repeats until centroids stop moving meaningfully.

Assumptions of K-Means

  • Clusters are roughly spherical / convex and similarly sized.
  • Clusters have similar density and variance.
  • Features are on comparable numeric scales (distance-based).
  • The number of clusters $K$ is known or can be estimated.
  • Clusters are roughly equally sized (extreme imbalance can distort results).
Section 3

Mathematical Foundation

Euclidean Distance

The straight-line distance between a data point $x_i$ and a centroid $\mu_j$ in $d$ dimensions:

$$d(x_i, \mu_j) = \sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{d}(x_{im} - \mu_{jm})^2}$$

Centroid Calculation

A centroid is simply the arithmetic mean of every point currently assigned to that cluster $C_j$:

$$\mu_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|}\sum_{x_i \in C_j} x_i$$

Objective Function — WCSS / Inertia

K-Means minimizes the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (also called inertia): the total squared distance between each point and its assigned centroid.

$$J = \sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{x_i \in C_j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2$$

Optimization Process

Minimizing $J$ jointly over cluster assignments and centroid positions is NP-hard, so K-Means uses Lloyd's algorithm: an iterative coordinate-descent-style heuristic that alternates two easy sub-problems —

  1. Fix centroids, optimize assignments: assign each point to its nearest centroid (this can only decrease or keep $J$ the same).
  2. Fix assignments, optimize centroids: the mean is the point that minimizes summed squared distance to a fixed set of points, so recomputing centroids as means can only decrease or keep $J$ the same.

Why K-Means Converges

Each of the two steps above is guaranteed to never increase $J$. Since $J \geq 0$ and there are only finitely many ways to partition $n$ points into $K$ groups, this monotonically non-increasing sequence must eventually stop changing — the algorithm always converges, though only to a local optimum, not necessarily the global one.
Section 4

The K-Means Algorithm

Step-by-Step

  1. Choose $K$ — the number of clusters.
  2. Initialize $K$ centroids (randomly, or via K-Means++).
  3. Assignment step: assign every point to its nearest centroid.
  4. Update step: recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points.
  5. Repeat steps 3–4 until centroids stop moving (or a max-iteration limit is hit).

Section 5

Choosing the Number of Clusters

Elbow Method — WCSS vs K

Silhouette Score vs K

Elbow Method

Plot WCSS (inertia) against $K$. As $K$ increases, WCSS always decreases — but the rate of improvement drops sharply after the "true" number of clusters, forming a visible bend or "elbow." That bend is a reasonable choice for $K$.

Silhouette Score

For each point $i$, let $a(i)$ be the mean distance to points in its own cluster, and $b(i)$ the mean distance to points in the nearest other cluster:

$$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}$$

Scores range from $-1$ to $1$; values near $1$ mean well-separated, tight clusters. Average $s(i)$ across all points and compare across candidate $K$ values — pick the $K$ that maximizes it.

MethodAdvantagesDisadvantages
Elbow MethodFast, intuitive, cheap to computeThe "elbow" can be subjective or absent on noisy data
Silhouette ScoreQuantitative, works even without an obvious elbow, evaluates separation and cohesionComputationally heavier ($O(n^2)$), less intuitive to visualize
Interview Tip: Use both together — the elbow narrows down a range, and the silhouette score picks the best candidate within it.
Section 6

Advantages and Limitations

Strengths

  • Simple to understand and implement
  • Scales well — near-linear time complexity
  • Guaranteed to converge
  • Works well on large, roughly spherical, well-separated clusters

Weaknesses

  • Must choose $K$ in advance
  • Assumes spherical, similarly-sized clusters
  • Sensitive to initial centroid placement
  • Sensitive to outliers (uses the mean)
  • Struggles with categorical or non-convex data

Sensitivity to Initialization

Because K-Means only finds a local optimum, a poor random start can trap it in a bad partition. K-Means++ mitigates this by picking initial centroids that are probabilistically spread apart — new centroids are chosen with probability proportional to squared distance from the nearest existing centroid — and scikit-learn also reruns the whole algorithm several times (n_init) and keeps the best result.

Effect of Outliers

Since a centroid is a mean, a single extreme outlier can drag it far from the true cluster center and inflate WCSS. Options: remove/cap outliers, use robust scaling, or switch to K-Medoids, which uses actual data points (medoids) instead of means.

When K-Means Should Not Be Used

  • Non-convex cluster shapes (rings, moons, spirals) — use DBSCAN or Spectral Clustering
  • Clusters of very different sizes/densities — use GMM or DBSCAN
  • Purely categorical data — use K-Modes
  • Heavy outlier contamination — use K-Medoids or robust pre-processing
  • Unknown / hard-to-estimate number of clusters — use hierarchical clustering or DBSCAN
Section 7

Distance Metrics

Every clustering algorithm needs a notion of "closeness." K-Means uses Euclidean distance by design, but it's worth understanding the alternatives.

Euclidean — straight line
Manhattan — grid path
Cosine — angle between vectors

Euclidean Distance

$$d_{euclid}(x,y) = \sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{d}(x_m - y_m)^2}$$

Measures straight-line distance. This is the metric K-Means' centroid-mean update is mathematically built around.

Manhattan Distance

$$d_{manhattan}(x,y) = \sum_{m=1}^{d}|x_m - y_m|$$

Sums absolute differences along each axis ("city block" distance). More robust to outliers than Euclidean, and pairs naturally with K-Medians rather than standard K-Means.

Cosine Distance (Discussion Only)

$$d_{cosine}(x,y) = 1 - \frac{x \cdot y}{\lVert x \rVert \lVert y \rVert}$$

Measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring magnitude — popular for text/TF-IDF data where document length shouldn't matter. Standard K-Means is not designed for this metric because its centroid-update step assumes Euclidean geometry; Spherical K-Means is the cosine-based variant.

Why Euclidean is Commonly Used

The K-Means objective function (WCSS) is derived directly from squared Euclidean distance, and the proof that centroid-as-mean minimizes that objective only holds for Euclidean geometry. Swapping in another metric without changing the update rule breaks the convergence guarantee.

6 · Elbow Method
wcss = []
for k in range(1, 11):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init='k-means++', n_init=10, random_state=42)
    km.fit(X_scaled)
    wcss.append(km.inertia_)

  • We loop k from 1 to 10, fitting a fresh KMeans model each time.
  • init='k-means++' uses smart initialization; n_init=10 reruns with 10 different seeds and keeps the best.
  • km.inertia_ is scikit-learn's attribute holding the final WCSS for that fit — we collect it to plot the elbow curve.
7 · Silhouette Score
sil_scores = []
for k in range(2, 11):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init='k-means++', n_init=10, random_state=42)
    labels = km.fit_predict(X_scaled)
    sil_scores.append(silhouette_score(X_scaled, labels))

plt.plot(range(2,11), sil_scores, marker='o', color='#f59e0b')
plt.xlabel('K'); plt.ylabel('Silhouette Score')
plt.show()
  • Silhouette needs at least 2 clusters, so the loop starts at k=2.
  • fit_predict fits the model and directly returns the cluster label for every row.
  • silhouette_score(X_scaled, labels) computes the mean silhouette coefficient across all points for that k.
8 · Train Final KMeans Model
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=5, init='k-means++', n_init=10, random_state=42)
kmeans.fit(X_scaled)
  • Based on the elbow bend and peak silhouette score, we pick K=5 for the Mall Customers dataset.
  • random_state=42 fixes the random seed so results are reproducible run to run.
9 · Predict Cluster Labels
y_kmeans = kmeans.predict(X_scaled)
  • predict assigns each scaled data point to its nearest of the 5 learned centroids, returning an integer label (0-4) per row.
10 · Add Cluster Column
df['Cluster'] = y_kmeans
df.head()
  • We attach the predicted labels back onto the original (unscaled) DataFrame so each customer row now carries its segment ID for reporting and business use.

Section 10

Practical Tips

Importance of Feature Scaling

K-Means is purely distance-based — an unscaled feature with a larger numeric range will dominate the distance calculation and silently determine the clustering, regardless of its real-world importance. Always scale (Standard or MinMax) before fitting.

Effect of random_state

K-Means' result depends on its random starting centroids. Fixing random_state makes results reproducible across runs — critical for debugging and for comparing model versions fairly.

The n_init Parameter

Controls how many times K-Means restarts with different centroid seeds, keeping the run with lowest inertia. Higher n_init reduces the risk of a bad local optimum at the cost of compute time.

Initialization Methods

init='random' picks $K$ random points as centroids (fast, less reliable). init='k-means++' (the default and recommended choice) spreads initial centroids apart probabilistically, converging faster and more reliably.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to scale features before fitting.
  • Choosing $K$ arbitrarily without the elbow/silhouette check.
  • Interpreting cluster centers in scaled space instead of inverse-transforming back to original units.
  • Applying K-Means to categorical variables directly (encode/avoid, or use K-Modes/K-Prototypes).
  • Ignoring outliers that distort centroid positions.
  • Assuming convergence to a global optimum — always compare against multiple n_init runs.
Section 11

Production Considerations

Saving Trained Models

import joblib
joblib.dump(kmeans, 'kmeans_model.pkl')
joblib.dump(scaler, 'scaler.pkl')
  • Persist both the fitted KMeans model and the StandardScaler — new data must go through the identical scaling before being assigned to a cluster.

Predicting Clusters for New Customers

scaler = joblib.load('scaler.pkl')
kmeans = joblib.load('kmeans_model.pkl')

new_customer = [[/* income */ 75, /* spending */ 82]]
new_scaled = scaler.transform(new_customer)
segment = kmeans.predict(new_scaled)
  • Load the saved artifacts, apply the already-fitted scaler's transform (not fit_transform) to avoid data leakage, then call predict to assign the new point to an existing cluster.

Retraining Strategies

  • Scheduled retraining: e.g., monthly/quarterly, as new customer data accumulates.
  • Trigger-based retraining: retrain when monitoring detects significant cluster drift (below).
  • Shadow evaluation: validate a newly retrained model against the current one before switching over.

Monitoring Cluster Drift

Over time, customer behavior shifts — the segments learned last year may no longer describe this year's population well. Practical monitoring signals:

  • Track the distribution of cluster sizes over time — a sudden imbalance signals drift.
  • Track average distance-to-assigned-centroid (inertia per point) — rising values suggest the model no longer fits incoming data well.
  • Recompute silhouette score periodically on fresh data batches.
  • Use statistical drift metrics (e.g., Population Stability Index) on the input features themselves.
Section 12

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • Objective: minimize WCSS / inertia.
  • Scaling: mandatory before fitting.
  • Initialization: use K-Means++ with several n_init restarts.
  • Choosing K: combine the Elbow Method with Silhouette Score.
  • Convergence: guaranteed, but only to a local optimum.

Best Practices

  • Always inverse-transform centroids before presenting results to stakeholders.
  • Fix random_state for reproducibility.
  • Monitor clusters in production — behavior drifts.

Final Quiz

Does K-Means guarantee a global optimum?

No. It can converge to local optima — which is exactly why we run it multiple times (n_init) and use smart initialization (K-Means++).

When to Use K-Means

Large datasets, roughly spherical and similarly-sized clusters, numeric features, a need for speed and interpretability, and a reasonable idea of how many groups you expect.
