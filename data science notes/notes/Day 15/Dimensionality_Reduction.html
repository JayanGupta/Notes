Dimensionality Reduction

PCA, LDA and ICA — three ways to compress features without losing the story.

Section 1

What is Dimensionality Reduction?

Imagine every feature in your dataset is one dimension. A dataset with 4 columns lives in 4-dimensional space; one with 500 columns (like a gene-expression panel or a bag-of-words vector) lives in 500-dimensional space. Dimensionality reduction takes that high-dimensional cloud of points and re-expresses it using fewer dimensions, while trying to keep whatever made the data interesting in the first place.

Why Too Many Features Hurts

  • More computation — every extra column costs memory and training time
  • Harder visualization — humans can only really see in 2D or 3D
  • Overfitting — models can latch onto noise when features vastly outnumber examples
  • Redundant information — many features are correlated and say the same thing twice
🔦

Fewer dimensions, same story — like a shadow that still shows the shape

Section 2

The Curse of Dimensionality

As the number of features grows, the space they live in grows exponentially larger. Your data doesn't grow with it, so the same number of points ends up scattered thinly across a nearly-empty space — distances stop being meaningful and every point starts looking roughly equally far from every other point.

Fraction of a hypercube's volume that lies within 10% of its surface, by dimension

In just a handful of dimensions, almost all of the volume sits near the outer shell — there's essentially no "middle" left. This is exactly why raw distance-based methods degrade in high dimensions, and why reducing dimensions first often helps.

Symptoms

  • Nearest-neighbor and farthest-neighbor distances converge
  • Models need exponentially more data to cover the space
  • Density-based and distance-based algorithms (like KNN, DBSCAN) become unreliable

The Fix

Reduce dimensions first, either by dropping uninformative features or — better — by combining correlated features into a smaller set of new ones that still carry the important structure. That's exactly what PCA, LDA and ICA do, each with a different notion of "important."

Section 4

PCA — Principal Component Analysis

PCA is unsupervised — it never looks at the class labels. It creates new features, called Principal Components, that are linear combinations of the originals, chosen to preserve as much variance (spread) in the data as possible.

What Are Principal Components?

Think of shining a flashlight on a 3D object and looking at its shadow on the wall. The shadow is only 2D, but if you angle the light correctly, that shadow still preserves the object's most important shape information. PCA finds the "best angle" mathematically — the direction that keeps the shadow as spread-out (informative) as possible.

The Algorithm, Step by Step

  1. Standardize the data (mean 0, variance 1) so no single feature dominates just because of its scale.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix of the standardized features.
  3. Find its eigenvectors and eigenvalues — the eigenvectors are the candidate directions, the eigenvalues tell you how much variance each direction captures.
  4. Keep the top-$k$ eigenvectors (highest eigenvalues) as your new axes and project the data onto them.
$$\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1}X^{\top}X \qquad \Sigma v = \lambda v$$

Here $\Sigma$ is the covariance matrix, and each eigenvector $v$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ defines one principal component — $\lambda$ is exactly the variance captured along that direction.

Iris projected onto the top 2 principal components

Notice setosa is still cleanly separated, and most of the spread now happens along one horizontal axis (PC1) — PCA rotated the data so that axis carries the most information, purely by chasing variance, without ever knowing the class labels.

Section 7

LDA — Linear Discriminant Analysis

LDA is supervised — it uses the class labels directly. Instead of asking "which direction has the most spread?" like PCA, it asks a different question entirely.

Why Labels? Instead of preserving variance, LDA asks: how can I separate the classes as much as possible? It looks for directions where points from the same class stay close together, while points from different classes end up far apart.

The Objective

$$J(w) = \frac{w^{\top}S_B\,w}{w^{\top}S_W\,w}$$

$S_B$ is the between-class scatter (how far apart the class means are) and $S_W$ is the within-class scatter (how spread out each class is internally). LDA finds the direction $w$ that maximizes this ratio — big separation between classes, small spread within each class.

Iris projected with LDA (using class labels)

Compare this to the PCA plot above — the three classes are now much more tightly grouped and clearly separated, because LDA was explicitly optimizing for exactly that.

Section 9

ICA — Independent Component Analysis

ICA is also unsupervised, but it optimizes for something different again: it looks for components that are statistically independent of each other, rather than components that maximize variance (PCA) or class separation (LDA).

The Cocktail Party Problem. Imagine several people speaking at once in a room, and several microphones scattered around recording mixed audio. Each microphone hears a different blend of every voice. ICA tries to "un-blend" the recordings and recover each person's original, independent voice signal.

Mixed signals (what the microphones hear)

Recovered independent sources

$$X = A\,S \qquad \hat{S} = W\,X$$

$S$ are the true, unknown independent sources; $A$ is the unknown mixing matrix that blends them into what we observe, $X$. ICA estimates an unmixing matrix $W \approx A^{-1}$ that recovers $\hat S$ — components that are as statistically independent (and non-Gaussian) as possible.

Iris projected with ICA

Real-World Applications

🧠

EEG / fMRI Analysis

Separating true brain signals from eye-blink and muscle artifacts.

🎙️

Audio Source Separation

Isolating individual voices or instruments from a mixed recording.

📈

Financial Signals

Disentangling independent driving factors behind correlated market data.

Requirement: ICA needs the underlying sources to be non-Gaussian and statistically independent — if the true sources are Gaussian, PCA and ICA become indistinguishable and ICA has nothing extra to exploit.
Section 10

Python Implementation

We'll run all three techniques on the same standardized Iris data so the comparison stays fair.

1 · Import Libraries & Load Data
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, FastICA
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis

data = load_iris()
X = data.data
y = data.target
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

plt.scatter(X[:,0], X[:,1], c=y, cmap='viridis')
plt.title("Original Dataset")
plt.show()
  • load_iris() returns 150 samples, 4 features, and integer class labels 0–2.
  • StandardScaler rescales every feature to mean 0, variance 1 — essential before any variance- or distance-based method.
2 · PCA
pca = PCA(n_components=2)
X_pca = pca.fit_transform(X_scaled)
plt.scatter(X_pca[:,0], X_pca[:,1], c=y, cmap='viridis')
plt.title("PCA")
plt.show()
print("Explained Variance Ratio:", pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
  • n_components=2 keeps only the top 2 principal components.
  • fit_transform both fits PCA on the data and returns the projected coordinates in one call.
  • explained_variance_ratio_ reports the fraction of total variance each kept component captures.
3 · LDA
lda = LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(n_components=2)
X_lda = lda.fit_transform(X_scaled, y)
plt.scatter(X_lda[:,0], X_lda[:,1], c=y, cmap='viridis')
plt.title("LDA")
plt.show()
  • Unlike PCA, fit_transform here takes both X_scaled and y — LDA needs the labels to compute between/within-class scatter.
  • For Iris (3 classes), LDA can produce at most 2 components (classes − 1).
4 · ICA
ica = FastICA(n_components=2, random_state=42)
X_ica = ica.fit_transform(X_scaled)
plt.scatter(X_ica[:,0], X_ica[:,1], c=y, cmap='viridis')
plt.title("ICA")
plt.show()
  • FastICA is scikit-learn's efficient implementation of ICA, using a fixed-point iteration algorithm.
  • random_state=42 makes the (otherwise randomly-initialized) unmixing search reproducible.
  • ICA doesn't rank components by importance the way PCA does — its 2 axes are just "the 2 most independent signals found."

Section 12

Summary

  • Dimensionality reduction compresses many features into fewer, while trying to keep what matters.
  • The curse of dimensionality is why fewer, better features often beat more, redundant ones.
  • PCA is unsupervised and chases variance — the flashlight-and-shadow analogy.
  • LDA is supervised and chases class separation, using labels directly.
  • ICA chases statistical independence — the cocktail-party un-mixing problem.
  • All three can be swapped in with a couple of lines of scikit-learn once your data is standardized.
